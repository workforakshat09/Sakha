Chapter 01 · Gita 1.47

The greatest archer alive sat down and could not move.

विसृज्य सशरं चापं शोकसंविग्नमानसः

In the middle of a war, Arjuna let his bow fall. The Gita doesn’t begin with a hero. It begins with a man who was stuck, afraid, and tired of what everyone expected of him.

Four small lessons

Every form.

  1. The lamp · Gita 6.19

    यथा दीपो निवातस्थो नेङ्गते सोपमा स्मृता ।
    योगिनो यतचित्तस्य युञ्जतो योगमात्मनः ॥

    “As a lamp in a windless place does not flicker: that is the mind of one who has gathered it in meditation.”

    Calm isn’t a life without wind. It’s a mind that doesn’t chase every gust.

    Try todayBefore you answer a message that upsets you, take three slow breaths. Let the flame settle, then reply.

  2. The wheel · Gita 2.14

    मात्रास्पर्शास्तु कौन्तेय शीतोष्णसुखदुःखदाः ।
    आगमापायिनोऽनित्यास्तांस्तितिक्षस्व भारत ॥

    “Heat and cold, pleasure and pain come and go. They do not last. Bear them patiently.”

    Every feeling arrives and turns away, like a wheel. The hard day you’re in is one turn, not the whole wheel.

    Try todayWhen something stings, say quietly, “this is passing.” Check how it feels an hour later.

  3. The feather · Gita 9.26

    पत्रं पुष्पं फलं तोयं यो मे भक्त्या प्रयच्छति ।
    तदहं भक्त्युपहृतमश्नामि प्रयतात्मनः ॥

    “Whoever offers me with love a leaf, a flower, a fruit or a little water, I accept it.”

    Krishna never asks for something grand. A small thing done with love is enough.

    Try todayDo one small thing with your whole heart: make someone tea, write one honest line, water a plant.

  4. Om · Gita 8.13

    ओमित्येकाक्षरं ब्रह्म व्याहरन्मामनुस्मरन् ।
    यः प्रयाति त्यजन्देहं स याति परमां गतिम् ॥

    “Whoever says Om, the one syllable that is Brahman, and remembers me at the end, reaches the highest goal.”

    Om is a sound you can come back to anywhere. One breath that holds everything.

    Try todaySit for one minute. Breathe in, and as you breathe out, hum “Om” softly. Three times.

Folio · The peacock feather

A feather in his hair, and a dance before the rain.

In paintings and in songs, Krishna wears a single peacock feather in his hair. Kings around him wore gold and jewels. He wore something a bird had let fall, which cost nothing at all.

Before the Gita was ever spoken, Krishna was a cowherd boy in Vrindavan who played the flute in the forest, and the Bhagavata Purana says the peacocks danced when they heard it. A peacock dances when the clouds gather, before a single drop has fallen, as if it already trusts what is coming.

The Gita asks for that kind of trust. Do your work with your whole heart, Krishna tells Arjuna, and let go of what it brings (2.47). Not because the result doesn’t matter, but because worrying has never made the rain come sooner.

No two eyes on a peacock’s feather are quite the same, and not one of them is a mistake. Neither are you.

Move your hand and he will follow. Press, and he will dance.
